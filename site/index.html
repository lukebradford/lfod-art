The rule

Every cell on an endless square grid is alive or dead, and looks at its eight neighbors. Then all of them change at once.

That is the whole universe. It is the opposite of company: a living cell keeps living only while nothing lives beside it, and life appears only where exactly two cells meet. Most beginnings explode outward at the speed of light into a widening diamond of chaos. A few, found by searching every small pattern, hold their shape forever.

Two cells, parting

Put two cells side by side. Twelve generations later they have become two identical free ships, flying apart at the speed of light, and they never stop.

Bestiary

Wakes

Some travelers leave something behind. A puffer is a moving pattern that lays down a trail as it goes, and these seven all fly at the speed of light. In this universe the trail is made of solitude: nearly everything they leave behind is a dot, one cell with no neighbors, which will therefore live forever. That is why their wakes are blue.

Each strip follows its puffer exactly, one generation per step, and all of them step together, so every wake streams past at the same speed: the speed of light. The whole trail is simulated, including the part that has scrolled out of view. Every few minutes they start over, and the counters jump back. Tap to pause. Patterns are from the LFoD Universe glossary. Their periods, speeds, populations and wakes are all checked by tests.

Rakes

A puffer leaves dots. A rake leaves ships. Here are four rakes from the glossary, and three of them fire moons backward. These four fly at the speed of light, and so does what they fire, so a moon and the rake that fired it part at twice the speed of light: as fast as any two things in this universe can separate. Each strip here flies with its rake, and the moons stream away behind it at two cells a generation.

Each strip follows its rake exactly, one generation per step, and all of them step together. Everything fired or left behind is simulated, including what has left the strip; the moons go on forever, and so do the free ships, which climb at forty-five degrees only because the camera is moving. Every few minutes they start over, and the counters jump back. Tap to pause. Patterns are from the LFoD Universe glossary. Their periods, speeds, populations, what each fires and leaves every period, and that nothing fired or left ever touches the rake or anything else again, are all checked by tests that follow each one for fifty periods.

Guns

Everything on this page that makes ships has to fly to do it: a rake moves at the speed of light and leaves its ships behind. A gun stands still. Here are two. Both fire moons, and both fire two ways at once, so each stands at the center of a line of light that lengthens at the speed of light from both ends.

Each strip holds its gun still, one generation per step, and both step together. Everything fired is simulated, including what has left the strip; a moon goes on forever. Each gun is shown exactly as its source gives it, the small one firing left and right, the laser up and down, and the line by the counter says how big its cells are drawn. Tap a strip to pause it, and open the small gun or the laser in the field at the top to watch it fire into the open.

Diamonds

Two cells side by side part into two ships. Touching at a corner, they blink forever. One cell apart, they vanish; in a line they are snake eyes, which flash into a plus first. But put them a knight’s move apart and they never stop. This pair is called evil eyes: of the 5 ways to place two cells close enough to meet, it is the only one that explodes.

Nothing moves faster than one cell per generation, and straight up, down, left and right the explosion does exactly that. Along the diagonals it cannot: a cell two diagonal steps beyond the edge could have only one living neighbor, and birth takes two. So the fastest growth this universe allows is a diamond, and every explosion here reaches that limit on all eight fronts within its first seventy generations, then holds it. Inside, about a fifth of the cells are alive at any moment, always different, never settling.

Three cells

GEN 0

Of the 31 ways to place three cells close enough to meet, 21 explode. Here are all of them, growing together from generation 0 at one scale. One rule, one speed limit, and no two alike. Tap one to watch it large.

“Close enough to meet” means every cell is within two of another, so they share a neighbor; rotations and reflections count once. The other ten three-cell placements die out or settle into a few small objects. The view above zooms out by halves as the diamond grows, so every cell stays a whole number of pixels, and it stops once the diamond fills the frame; the explosion itself never stops. Each frame is computed exactly on an unbounded plane by a bit-packed engine (32 cells per machine word), which is tested cell for cell against the one that draws everything else. The counts, the fates, the speeds and the density are all checked by tests.

Four cells

GEN 0

Add one more cell and there are 430 ways to place four close enough to meet. 360 of them explode. Of the rest, 26 simply vanish and 44 settle into something that lasts. Here are all 44, from generation 0.

Four are already what they will be: the moon, the only four cells that already fly, and three oscillators of period four, the anchor, the lever and the radar. One other placement makes a moon of its own, throwing off two dots as it goes. Seven part into two free ships flying apart, as the two-cell domino does (one of them leaves four checkers as well). The other 29 leave nothing but dots and checkers: a dot is a cell alone, and a checker is two cells taking turns.

The more cells, the less often there is peace: one pair in 5 explodes, 21 of 31 triples, and 430 placements of four leave 70 that don’t. Each tile above runs exactly, on an unbounded plane, for forty generations; whatever flies out of its frame keeps flying. A caption turns dark at the generation it becomes true. Which objects each placement leaves, the generation it settles by, that the pieces never meet again, and the count of every case are all checked by tests that enumerate the placements independently of this page.

Five cells

GEN 0

One more cell, and there are 6,634 ways to place five close enough to meet. 6,151 explode, 59 vanish and 415 settle. The last nine do something no smaller placement does: they grow forever and never explode. Each becomes a double dot puffer, which flies off at the speed of light and leaves a dot behind every two generations. One of them makes two, flying apart.

The double dot puffer is the smallest known puffer: six cells, in the form the glossary gives. These start with five and grow into it within 9 generations. In this universe a trail is a line of dots, each a cell alone, so each one lives forever.

All 483

Here is every five-cell placement that doesn’t explode, grouped by what it becomes. Tap one to watch it. Five cells bring the first ant, the period-6 oscillator found by Josh Ball, from a single placement. They also bring the first lone free ship: before now, free ships were only ever born in pairs flying apart. Among the ones that vanish is the plus.

Peace keeps getting rarer as cells are added. Four pairs in 5 don’t explode, then 10 triples in 31, 70 placements of four in 430, and now 483 in 6,634, about one in fourteen. One explosion takes its time: at generation 40 it is still only 25 cells, and then it goes like all the rest. The puffers’ strips are drawn on a fixed plane, and each run lasts until the last puffer reaches the right edge. The pair’s puffers leave their strip halfway through, and like everything else here they keep going. Every count, fate and generation on this page is checked by tests that enumerate all 6,634 placements independently of it and run each one. The puffers are checked over fifty periods: every cell they leave behind is a dot, and nothing else ever changes.

Slower than light

Everything that moves on this page so far moves at the speed of light, one cell every generation. Every ship this site’s searches have turned up flies at that speed. Going slower is hard. The glossary of the Live Free or Die Universe knows 21 ships that do, most of them hundreds of cells. Here is the smallest at each speed, racing from one starting line.

The race

GEN 0

At generation 0, every ship’s nose is on the same column. The shaded side is beyond the light front, which moves one column every generation: whatever started behind that line can never be ahead of it. The moon rides it exactly. The rest fall behind, each at its own speed, and by the time the moon reaches the end of the track, the decade ship has gone a fifth of the way.

Watch the last lane. The decade ship is one of only two ships known whose nose ever moves backward. Every five generations it falls back two cells, then creeps forward three. The other is the quintessential ship, which goes at the same speed with the same front end. No ship at the speed of light could do it: to keep up with light, a nose has to gain a cell every single generation.

Diagonal

Straight ahead, a ship can keep up with light. Along a diagonal it can’t: a cell two diagonal steps beyond the leading edge can only ever have one living neighbor, and birth takes two, so nothing can advance diagonally faster than one step every two generations. That limit is why every explosion is a diamond. The three diagonal ships known go half as fast as it allows, one step every four generations. The shaded corner is beyond the diagonal limit, where no cell can be alive.

Every slow ship

All 21, fastest first. Twelve go at half the speed of light; one each at two fifths, a third and a quarter; three at a quarter along a diagonal; and three at a fifth. The smallest have 36 cells. Twelve were found by Josh Ball. Tap one to watch it, the camera flying with it.
