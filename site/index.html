The rule

Every cell on an endless square grid is alive or dead, and looks at its eight neighbours. Then all of them change at once.

That is the whole universe. It is the opposite of company: a living cell keeps living only while nothing lives beside it, and life appears only where exactly two cells meet. Most beginnings explode outward at the speed of light into a widening diamond of chaos. A few, found by searching every small pattern, hold their shape forever.

Two cells, parting

Put two cells side by side. Twelve generations later they have become two identical free ships, flying apart at the speed of light, and they never stop.

Wakes

Some travellers leave something behind. A puffer flies at the speed of light and lays down a trail as it goes. In this universe the trail is made of solitude: nearly everything these seven leave behind is a dot, one cell with no neighbours, which will therefore live forever. That is why their wakes are red.

Each strip follows its puffer exactly, one generation per step, and all of them step together, so every wake streams past at the same speed: the speed of light. The whole trail is simulated, including the part that has scrolled out of view. Every few minutes they start over, and the counters jump back. Tap to pause. Patterns are from the LFoD Universe glossary. Their periods, speeds, populations and wakes are all checked by tests.

Collisions

Nothing can catch a moon: it already moves at the speed of light. But things can stand in its way, or come the other way. Send one at a dot, a checker or another moon, and what happens depends only on how many rows off-centre it hits and, against anything that moves or blinks, whether the gap between them is even or odd. Here is every way it can go.

The moon always enters from the left with its top row on row 0; the offset is the row where the target's top cell sits. Rows further out than those listed miss entirely, and the two carry on as if nothing were there. Of the 48 collisions that touch, 26 explode. Each run plays from generation 0 until the result has settled and anything still moving has left the frame; an explosion stops where it reaches the edge, still growing. Every outcome, the generation it settles by, and that the pieces never meet again, is checked by a test.

Diamonds

Two cells side by side part into two ships. Touching at a corner, they blink forever. One cell apart, they vanish; in a line they are snake eyes, which flash into a plus first. But put them a knight’s move apart and they never stop. This pair is called evil eyes: of the 5 ways to place two cells close enough to meet, it is the only one that explodes.

Nothing moves faster than one cell per generation, and straight up, down, left and right the explosion does exactly that. Along the diagonals it cannot: a cell two diagonal steps beyond the edge could have only one living neighbour, and birth takes two. So the fastest growth this universe allows is a diamond, and every explosion here reaches that limit on all eight fronts within its first seventy generations, then holds it. Inside, about a fifth of the cells are alive at any moment, always different, never settling.

Four cells

Add one more cell and there are 430 ways to place four close enough to meet. 360 of them explode. Of the rest, 26 simply vanish and 44 settle into something that lasts. Here are all 44, from generation 0.

Four are already what they will be: the moon, the only four cells that already fly, and three oscillators of period four, the anchor, the lever and the radar. One other placement makes a moon of its own, throwing off two dots as it goes. Seven part into two free ships flying apart, as the two-cell domino does (one of them leaves four checkers as well). The other 29 leave nothing but dots and checkers: a dot is a cell alone, and a checker is two cells taking turns.

The more cells, the less often there is peace: one pair in 5 explodes, 21 of 31 triples, and 430 placements of four leave 70 that don’t. Each tile above runs exactly, on an unbounded plane, for forty generations; whatever flies out of its frame keeps flying. A caption turns dark at the generation it becomes true. Which objects each placement leaves, the generation it settles by, that the pieces never meet again, and the count of every case are all checked by tests that enumerate the placements independently of this page.

Five cells

gen 0

One more cell, and there are 6,634 ways to place five close enough to meet. 6,151 explode, 59 vanish and 415 settle. The last nine do something no smaller placement does: they grow forever and never explode. Each becomes a double dot puffer, which flies off at the speed of light and leaves a dot behind every two generations. One of them makes two, flying apart.

The double dot puffer is the smallest known puffer: six cells, in the form the glossary gives. These start with five and grow into it within 9 generations. In this universe a trail is a line of dots, each a cell alone, so each one lives forever. Four cells can grow forever too, but only if they start too far apart to meet: see two dominoes, below.

Two dominoes

A domino, two cells side by side, parts into two free ships that fly apart forever. Put down two dominoes and four ships fly out. What becomes of them depends only on where the second domino sits. Here is every place it can go, out to 12 rows across and 36 cells along, and what happens.

Close together, they almost always explode. In line, or nearly (up to five rows across), the two inner ships fly straight at each other. Usually that explodes too, but at the right distances the two ships annihilate, or leave a few dots and checkers, and the outer two fly away. Six to eight rows across, every meeting explodes. From twelve rows on, they never touch: a free ship never strays more than four rows beyond its domino, so at least two empty rows always lie between them, and no cell can see across two. At ten and eleven rows the ships only just brush past each other. Often that explodes too, but when it doesn’t, all four fly on, sometimes leaving a dot or a checker behind.

And at eleven rows across and two along, the ships flying the same way never get free of each other. Neither ship changes in the slightest: at every generation each domino is exactly what it would be alone. But every six generations their sparks reach the row halfway between them at the same moment, and a cell is born there, alone, so it lives forever. The glossary calls this dueling dominoes, “the smallest pattern that exhibits infinite linear growth”. Of all 479 placements here, it is the only one that grows. Each of its puffers is just two free ships flying side by side, and there are more ways for two ships to do that: see side by side, below.

Mirror images are the same placement, so the map is symmetric about the first domino. Of the 479, 347 explode, 65 never touch, and 66 touch and settle. Beyond 24 cells along, the map repeats every 12: the inner ships meet six generations later, in the same phase, and the pairs flying the same way are too far apart to touch. Each run plays from generation 0 on an unbounded plane until the result has settled and the ships have left the frame; an explosion stops where it reaches the edge, still growing. Every square, the generation each result is reached by, the symmetry, the repeat (checked out to 72 cells along), and every dot dueling dominoes lays down (for 700 generations) are checked by tests that run each placement and compare it with the two dominoes run alone.

Side by side

Dueling dominoes is four free ships flying in two pairs. A free ship moves at the speed of light, so two flying the same way can never gain on each other or fall behind: whatever passes between them happens again six generations later, and again, forever, unless it destroys them. So here is every way to set a second free ship beside a first, flying the same way, in any of its six phases, anywhere close enough to touch. Nearly every one explodes. These nine fly on together forever.

The first four are flotillas: two ships that hold together forever and leave nothing behind. Every six generations their sparks reach across the gap and touch. For one, two or three generations something passes between them; then it is gone without a trace. The first is exactly two free ships, each just as it would be alone, in five generations out of every six.

    The other five are puffers. Where their sparks touch, something is left behind that is alone, and so can never die: a dot or two, or a checker, every six generations. The second is the puffer dueling dominoes makes two of; the others do not appear among the glossary’s patterns. In all nine, neither ship is ever changed for good: in at least three generations of every six, each is exactly what it would be alone.

    Every placement

    Of the 1,429 places where the second ship touches the first, 1,369 explode. In 45 more, a single free ship flies on, sometimes with dots, checkers or a moon. Two ships never come through a meeting and part as strangers: they explode, only one ship comes out, or they stay together forever. Choose the second ship’s phase, then a place for it.

    The first ship is in its smallest form, the glossary’s twelve cells. Phase k means the second is k generations further through its six-generation cycle; along is how far ahead its path runs. A lone free ship never strays outside a band ten rows tall and fifteen columns long, so beyond 16 along or 11 across two empty rows or columns always lie between the two ships, no cell can see both, and they never touch. Mirror images count once, so there are 1,938 placements, and 509 of them never touch. The fleet above is one pattern, simulated exactly, its lanes far enough apart that they can never meet; the camera moves with it at the speed of light, and the trails stream out behind. Every square, the generation each result is reached by, which flotillas and puffers are the same, how each one behaves generation by generation, and that the fleet’s lanes never meet are checked by tests that run every placement.
