The rule

Every cell on an endless square grid is alive or dead, and looks at its eight neighbours. Then all of them change at once.

That is the whole universe. It is the opposite of company: a living cell keeps living only while nothing lives beside it, and life appears only where exactly two cells meet. Most beginnings explode outward at the speed of light into a widening diamond of chaos. A few, found by searching every small pattern, hold their shape forever.

Two cells, parting

Put two cells side by side. Twelve generations later they have become two identical free ships, flying apart at the speed of light, and they never stop.

Bestiary

Everything here turned up when every pattern in a four-by-four box, and every pattern of up to seven cells in a five-by-five box, was run until it settled. Every claim on each card is checked by a test against the same engine that draws it. Names are the established ones, from the glossary of the Live Free or Die Universe, the rule's original pattern collection, matched to these patterns by simulation.

Wakes

Some travellers leave something behind. A puffer flies at the speed of light and lays down a trail as it goes. In this universe the trail is made of solitude: nearly everything these seven leave behind is a dot, one cell with no neighbours, which will therefore live forever. That is why their wakes are red.

Each strip follows its puffer exactly, one generation per step, and all of them step together, so every wake streams past at the same speed: the speed of light. The whole trail is simulated, including the part that has scrolled out of view. Every few minutes they start over, and the counters jump back. Tap to pause. Patterns are from the LFoD Universe glossary. Their periods, speeds, populations and wakes are all checked by tests.

Collisions

Nothing can catch a moon: it already moves at the speed of light. But things can stand in its way, or come the other way. Send one at a dot, a checker or another moon, and what happens depends only on how many rows off-centre it hits and, against anything that moves or blinks, whether the gap between them is even or odd. Here is every way it can go.

The moon always enters from the left with its top row on row 0; the offset is the row where the target's top cell sits. Rows further out than those listed miss entirely, and the two carry on as if nothing were there. Of the 48 collisions that touch, 26 explode. Each run plays from generation 0 until the result has settled and anything still moving has left the frame; an explosion stops where it reaches the edge, still growing. Every outcome, the generation it settles by, and that the pieces never meet again, is checked by a test.

Diamonds

Two cells side by side part into two ships. Touching at a corner, they blink forever. One cell apart, they vanish. But put them a knight’s move apart and they never stop. This pair is called evil eyes: of the 5 ways to place two cells close enough to meet, it is the only one that explodes.

Nothing moves faster than one cell per generation, and straight up, down, left and right the explosion does exactly that. Along the diagonals it cannot: a cell two diagonal steps beyond the edge could have only one living neighbour, and birth takes two. So the fastest growth this universe allows is a diamond, and every explosion here reaches that limit on all eight fronts within its first seventy generations, then holds it. Inside, about a fifth of the cells are alive at any moment, always different, never settling.

Three cells

gen 0

Of the 31 ways to place three cells close enough to meet, 21 explode. Here are all of them, growing together from generation 0 at one scale. One rule, one speed limit, and no two alike. Tap one to watch it large.

“Close enough to meet” means every cell is within two of another, so they share a neighbour; rotations and reflections count once. The other ten three-cell placements die out or settle into a few small objects. The view above zooms out by halves as the diamond grows, so every cell stays a whole number of pixels, and it stops once the diamond fills the frame; the explosion itself never stops. Each frame is computed exactly on an unbounded plane by a bit-packed engine (32 cells per machine word), which is tested cell for cell against the one that draws everything else. The counts, the fates, the speeds and the density are all checked by tests.

Four cells

gen 0

Add one more cell and there are 430 ways to place four close enough to meet. 360 of them explode. Of the rest, 26 simply vanish and 44 settle into something that lasts. Here are all 44, from generation 0.

Four are already what they will be: the moon, the only four cells that already fly, and three oscillators of period four, the anchor, the lever and the radar. One other placement makes a moon of its own, throwing off two dots as it goes. Seven part into two free ships flying apart, as the two-cell domino does (one of them leaves four checkers as well). The other 29 leave nothing but dots and checkers: a dot is a cell alone, and a checker is two cells taking turns.

The more cells, the less often there is peace: one pair in 5 explodes, 21 of 31 triples, and 430 placements of four leave 70 that don’t. Each tile above runs exactly, on an unbounded plane, for forty generations; whatever flies out of its frame keeps flying. A caption turns dark at the generation it becomes true. Which objects each placement leaves, the generation it settles by, that the pieces never meet again, and the count of every case are all checked by tests that enumerate the placements independently of this page.
